1. Core modal verbs

Modal verbs are auxiliary verbs. They are followed by the base form of the main verb: She can drive. They do not normally take -s, do/does, or to before the main verb.

Modal / expressionMain functionsExample
canability; permission; possibilityShe can solve complex problems.
couldpast/general ability; polite permission/request; possibilityCould I use your notes?
mayformal permission; possibilityYou may leave once you have finished.
mightpossibility, usually less certainWe might need more time.
muststrong obligation; logical deductionYou must wear a helmet. / She must be exhausted.
should / ought toadvice; expectation; mild obligationYou should check the figures.
had betterstrong advice, often with an implied consequenceYou'd better save your work.
have toexternal obligation / necessityWe have to submit the form today.
neednecessity; sometimes used as a modal in questions/negativesYou needn't bring anything.
be able toability, especially where a modal form is unavailableShe was able to finish on time.
be allowed topermissionStudents are allowed to use dictionaries.
be permitted toformal permissionVisitors are permitted to take photographs.
be supposed toexpected arrangement / dutyYou are supposed to submit it by Friday.

2. Permission, obligation and necessity

can / could / may can express permission. May is more formal; could is common for polite requests.
“Can I use your charger?” — “Yes, of course.”
“Could I leave a little early?” — a polite request.
“You may begin when you are ready.” — formal permission.
must vs have to: both express obligation. Must often presents the speaker's strong requirement; have to commonly refers to an external rule or circumstance.
You must back up your files. (speaker's strong instruction)
We have to wear ID badges at school. (institutional requirement)
mustn't ≠ don't have to. Mustn't means “it is prohibited”. Don't have to means “it is not necessary”.
You mustn't disclose the password. = It is forbidden.
You don't have to print the article. = It is optional.
needn't / don't need to: both mean that something is unnecessary.
You needn't wait for me.
You don't need to bring a calculator.
needn't have + past participle: an action happened, but it was unnecessary.
You needn't have brought any food; there was plenty here.
didn't need to + infinitive: it was not necessary. Depending on context, the action may or may not have happened.
We didn't need to book in advance because the restaurant was empty.

3. Advice: should, ought to and had better

You should revise the key terms before the exam.
You ought to tell her what happened.
You'd better leave now, or you'll miss the last train.

Had better is stronger and often suggests a warning or an undesirable consequence.

4. Ability and permission across tenses

MeaningUseful formExample
present abilitycan / am-is-are able toI can analyse the data.
past general abilitycould / was-were able toAt ten, she could already read fluently.
successful ability on one occasionwas/were able toDespite the delay, we were able to catch the train.
permission nowcan / may / be allowed toWe are allowed to use the library.
permission in another tensebe allowed/permitted toThey were permitted to enter.
C1 nuance: could is natural for general past ability. For a specific successful event, was/were able to often makes the successful outcome explicit.

5. Speculation and deduction

These forms express different degrees of certainty. Context matters: the same modal can express different meanings in different sentences.

FormTypical meaningExample
must + infinitivestrong deduction about the presentShe must be at work; her car is outside.
can't / couldn't + infinitivestrong negative deductionHe can't be serious. / They couldn't have known.
may / might / could + infinitivepossibilityThey might be stuck in traffic.
must have + past participlestrong deduction about the pastShe must have forgotten.
can't/couldn't have + past participlestrong negative deduction about the pastHe can't have taken the keys.
may/might/could have + past participlepast possibilityThey may have missed the announcement.
should/ought to have + past participleexpected action/result, sometimes with criticismShe should have arrived by now.
be bound tovery strong expectationYou're bound to enjoy the exhibition.
be sure tostrong expectation/certaintyShe's sure to notice the difference.
be likely toprobablePrices are likely to rise.
be unlikely tonot probableHe is unlikely to agree.
definitely / probablyadverbs of certainty/probabilityThey will probably arrive late.
Continuous infinitive: modal + be + -ing can refer to an action in progress now.
He must be working late tonight.
Perfect continuous: modal + have been + -ing can refer to an activity continuing up to a past or present point.
They must have been waiting for hours.
Modal + perfect infinitive is crucial for past speculation: must have left, might have forgotten, can't have seen.
